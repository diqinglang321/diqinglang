## Scientific interpretation

- Energy figures are mean CHANGE from baseline at six months, not prescribed calories or absolute daily intake. The adjusted −244 kcal/day contrast is reported separately from the two group means.
- Lower energy intake could mediate programme effectiveness. The presentation does not call it failed randomisation or recommend indiscriminately adjusting it away.
- No objective ketone verification is reported. This does not mean nobody achieved ketosis.
- Six-month follow-up is 59/80; the direction of any attrition bias is unknown. Some exit reasons were reported. ITT is claimed; missing-outcome handling is not clearly described in the supplied report.
- The cohort pictogram uses one generic symbol per participant; it does not encode individual patient outcomes or dropout reasons.
- Single-centre recruitment and eligibility restrictions limit extrapolation; they do not show that other populations would fail to benefit.

## Speaker script and pacing

### 01. A fair start. (00:00–00:10)

First, the trial has a fair starting point: eighty adults were randomized, stratified by sex and BMI, with third-party allocation using opaque envelopes.

### 02. Same support. Measured outcomes. (00:10–00:20)

Both groups received workshops, the app and dietitian support. Six-month weight change was the primary outcome, measured using a calibrated scale.

### 03. The calorie gap. (00:20–00:38)

However, energy intake also changed. At six months, reported intake fell by seven hundred and four calories in HKD and four hundred and twenty-three in ERD. The adjusted difference in change was two hundred and forty-four calories per day.

### 04. Two things changed. (00:38–00:55)

So the trial compares whole programmes; it cannot isolate diet composition from lower energy intake. Eating less may be a mediator, rather than failed randomisation. Isocaloric controlled feeding would help isolate composition.

### 05. Assigned is not verified. (00:55–01:13)

Second, the carbohydrate target was fifty grams or less, assessed through self-reported food diaries. But assignment to a ketogenic diet does not prove sustained ketosis: objective ketone verification was not reported.

### 06. Measure the missing link. (01:13–01:30)

Repeated blood or urine ketone measurements would strengthen this link. Researchers should also report how many participants meet carbohydrate and ketosis targets, and how adherence changes over time.

### 07. Who stays in the picture? (01:30–01:36)

Third, consider follow-up. All eighty participants began the randomized comparison.

### 08. Who stays in the picture? (01:36–01:50)

Only fifty-nine completed six-month measurements, and fifty-one completed one year. If missing participants had different outcomes, the comparison could be biased; the direction is uncertain.

### 09. Keep measuring, even after stopping. (01:50–02:05)

Even after stopping the diet, participants should still be followed. Exit reasons should be documented more fully. ITT was claimed, but missing-outcome handling was unclear; my teammate covers the statistical details.

### 10. One hospital is not everyone. (02:05–02:20)

Finally, this was one Singapore hospital’s healthcare staff, about eighty-six percent women, with smartphone and English requirements. Diverse multicentre recruitment would test broader applicability.

### 11. A useful trial. A bounded conclusion. (02:20–02:30)

Overall, the design can evaluate a supported dietary programme, but leaves questions about mechanism, missing outcomes and who else may benefit.
