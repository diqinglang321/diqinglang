# Scientific explanation — revision 2

This companion explains the logic behind the short presentation. It is not an extra section to read aloud. The presentation stays with the supplied paper and does not perform a new statistical analysis.

## 1. What exactly is wrong with the calorie gap?

**Paper fact.** Both groups received energy-restriction advice, but reported mean energy-intake change at six months differed: HKD −704 kcal/day and ERD −423 kcal/day (Table 5). These are changes from baseline, not absolute intake or calories prescribed. The table also reports an adjusted contrast in change of −244 kcal/day; that adjusted estimate is not simply the subtraction of the two displayed group means. The shorter main presentation omits this extra statistical number to keep attention on the inference.

**Causal logic.** Random assignment was to complete supported dietary programmes, not to ketosis alone while every other exposure was held constant. There are several plausible paths from assigned HKD advice to weight loss: changes in diet composition may influence physiological processes; changes in satiety may lead to lower energy intake; and ketosis, if it occurs, may itself influence appetite and intake. Thus the displayed routes are non-exhaustive and may operate together. Their relative contribution was not identified by the trial.

**Why this is not automatically confounding.** A baseline confounder is conceptually different from a post-assignment pathway. If HKD causes lower intake and lower intake causes weight loss, intake is a mediator of the programme effect. That can be a real part of why the programme helps. It does not itself show failed randomisation or bias in the total assignment comparison. Nor does it follow that investigators should automatically adjust away the observed intake difference: that would change the causal question, and the necessary mediation assumptions have not been established here.

**The precise limitation.** The trial can inform a programme-level comparison, subject to its other limitations. It cannot establish that the extra loss represents a calorie-independent effect of carbohydrate restriction or ketosis. Calling all of the benefit a unique metabolic advantage would overstate the causal attribution. The reported-intake data are self-reported, so they also do not provide a precise physiological energy-balance experiment. The diagram uses dashed arrows to make clear that the displayed mediation paths are hypotheses, not measured causal estimates.

## 2. What does unverified ketosis prevent us from concluding?

**Paper fact.** The dietary target was no more than 50 g/day net carbohydrate. Intake was assessed using self-reported food diaries (Methods 2.3 and 2.7). The paper does not report objective verification with blood or urine ketones; its Discussion proposes such measurements for future research.

**The exposure distinction.** A prescription is what a participant is asked to do. A diary is an account of intake. Nutritional ketosis is a metabolic state. These are related concepts, but one does not establish the others. In particular, a group label does not tell us which participants reached ketosis, whether they remained there, or how long the exposure lasted.

**Concrete consequence.** Observed weight loss cannot solve the missing exposure information because more than one pathway can produce weight loss, including lower energy intake. Reasoning backwards from weight loss to ketosis would therefore be unjustified. The trial estimates the consequences of assignment to HKD advice rather than an experimentally isolated effect of verified ketosis. This is a limitation of intervention-fidelity/exposure verification and mechanistic inference. It does not establish that nobody achieved ketosis, that the programme is ineffective, or that the randomized assignment comparison automatically fails. If assignment were treated as if it were measured ketosis in an exposure analysis, exposure misclassification would be a concern; its direction cannot be determined from this report.

## 3. How could missing weights change the result?

**Paper fact.** There were 80 randomized participants: 41 HKD and 39 ERD. At six months, 29 HKD and 30 ERD participants completed measurements, giving 59 measured and 21 without six-month completion: 12 HKD and 9 ERD (Figure 1 and Table 2). Every pictogram represents one participant; the symbols do not contain individual outcomes. Figure 1 reports some exit reasons, including pregnancy, so we do not assume all departures reflect dietary difficulty.

**The selection mechanism.** Randomisation makes the original assigned groups comparable in expectation. But a subset selected later by availability of outcomes need not remain comparable. If availability is associated with response, the observed weights may represent a different mix of responders from the full assigned group. This is more than merely having a smaller sample: it can change who contributes to the estimated comparison.

**Conditional example.** Suppose the missing HKD participants lost less weight than the HKD participants who were measured. A completer-only HKD mean could then show more weight loss than the mean among everyone assigned HKD. If this selection is stronger in HKD than in ERD, the apparent HKD advantage could be exaggerated. Conversely, if poorer responders were preferentially missing from ERD, the observed control group could look better, shrinking the apparent HKD advantage. Other patterns are possible. These are explanations of potential attrition bias, not findings about the actual missing participants. The paper does not provide the missing weight outcomes needed to determine the direction or magnitude of such a bias.

**What the ITT statement does and does not tell us.** The authors describe their analysis as intention-to-treat, but the supplied report does not clearly explain how missing outcomes were handled. Retaining original group labels is important, but it does not restore unmeasured weights. We therefore cannot infer from the label alone that the reported effect is free from attrition bias. This is a reporting uncertainty; it is not proof that the investigators performed no missing-data procedure.

**Why ongoing measurement matters.** People who stop the diet still belong to the randomized assignment comparison. Continuing to collect their weights where possible preserves information about the effect of offering the programme, including difficulty adhering to it. Remaining missingness requires transparent methods and assumptions; detailed imputation and sensitivity-analysis choices can be addressed by the statistics presenter. This short principle is integrated into the explanation, not presented as a separate improvement checklist.

## Overall judgement

The calorie gap and unverified ketosis primarily limit mechanistic attribution. Missing outcome data can additionally threaten the estimated programme contrast through selection, depending on the unobserved responses and analysis. These are distinct problems and should not all be labelled “confounding.”
