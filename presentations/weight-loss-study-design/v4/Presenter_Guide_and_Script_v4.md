## Pacing and speaker script

### 01. Why this design deserves credit. — 00:00–00:22

Four features strengthen the comparison. Stratified randomisation helps balance sex and BMI. Concealed allocation reduces opportunities to select participants knowing their next group. Shared support makes unequal attention a less likely explanation. Calibrated weighing reduces reliance on participants’ own weight reports.

### 02. A calorie gap accompanied the result. — 00:22–00:34

First, the weight-loss mechanism was not isolated. Both groups received calorie-restriction advice, yet reported intake fell more in HKD. These were not experimentally matched calorie intakes.

### 03. Did ketosis explain the extra loss? — 00:34–00:47

Low-carbohydrate advice was intended to induce ketosis, which might contribute to weight loss. But no objective ketone verification was reported, so this metabolic state was unconfirmed.

### 04. Calories offer another explanation. — 00:47–00:59

Lower calorie intake offers another explanation. These routes may overlap: ketosis, if present, might influence appetite and intake. The trial did not isolate their contributions.

### 05. Two gaps. One mechanism limitation. — 00:59–01:12

Together, unverified ketosis and unmatched calories limit one mechanism claim. The result concerns the whole programme, not an established ketosis-specific benefit independent of calorie intake.

### 06. Who stays in the picture? — 01:12–01:17

Second, consider follow-up: eighty participants entered.

### 07. Who stays in the picture? — 01:17–01:25

Only fifty-nine completed six-month measurements; twenty-one weights were unavailable. Missing measurement does not establish dietary non-adherence.

### 08. The missing weights could matter. — 01:25–01:37

Twelve HKD weights were missing. These participants could have lost less, the same, or more weight than those measured, changing the group estimate.

### 09. Missing weights may bias the result. — 01:37–01:52

If poorer HKD responders were disproportionately missing, the observed advantage could look greater. Other patterns could shrink it. This is potential attrition bias; its direction and the handling of missing outcomes remain unclear.

### 10. Who does this sample represent? — 01:52–02:06

Third, recruitment involved one hospital’s staff, about eighty-six percent women, with English and smartphone requirements. Random assignment does not make that sample representative of all adults with obesity.

### 11. Would it work the same elsewhere? — 02:06–02:22

The programme also included seven workshops and app-based dietitian coaching. Other people, or those receiving less support, may respond differently. Their benefit is untested, not proven absent.

### 12. Three limits. Three different questions. — 02:22–02:30

These are three distinct questions: why it works, how reliable the estimate is, and whom it applies to.

## Scientific boundaries

### Mechanism

Both groups received energy-restriction advice, but the report does not describe controlled feeding ensuring equal calorie intake. The −704 and −423 kcal/day values are reported changes from baseline, not prescribed or absolute calorie intakes. Greater reduction in HKD does not establish that all extra weight loss was caused by calories, nor does it establish a failed randomisation. Lower intake could mediate the programme effect. Ketosis, if achieved, might itself affect appetite and intake, so the pictured routes need not be independent or mutually exclusive. Neither objective ketone verification nor a calorie-independent ketosis effect was established. Even equal calories alone would not uniquely prove ketosis as the cause.

### Missing outcomes

80 randomized participants became 59 with six-month measurements: HKD 29/41 and ERD 30/39. Missing weights total 21, comprising 12 HKD and 9 ERD. Missing measurement does not prove non-adherence. If poorer HKD responders were disproportionately missing, the measured HKD subset could look more successful; other patterns could shrink the contrast. This is a hypothetical bias mechanism, not a finding about the actual missing participants. The report does not clearly describe missing-outcome handling. The independent ITT page and terminology remain absent from the main presentation.

### Generalisability

Recruitment was from healthcare staff at one Singapore hospital. 69/80 participants were women (86.25%, rounded to 86% in the slide); only 11 were men. English literacy and smartphone access were required, and some diseases were excluded. These may be sensible eligibility criteria, but they narrow the population directly represented. Random assignment supports comparison inside the sample; it does not create a random sample of all adults with obesity. No claim is made that effects necessarily differ by sex or occupation.

Participants received seven workshops and app-based dietitian coaching over six months. Similar results in other populations or less-supported use cannot be assumed. This is not evidence of no benefit outside the study, and it does not imply that the two study arms had unequal support.
