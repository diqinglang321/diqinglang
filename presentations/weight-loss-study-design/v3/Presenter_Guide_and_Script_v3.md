# Revision 3 — presenter guide and script

## Changes in this version

- The strengths opening now evaluates four features. Each feature has a native editable graphic, a heading and a why-it-helps explanation. Four controlled reveals keep the presenter on evaluation rather than reintroducing the study.
- The branched causal diagram is replaced with a simple composition-versus-reported-calorie comparison. These factors were not experimentally isolated; lower intake may be part of the programme benefit.
- Ketosis remains unverified. Neither the carbohydrate prescription nor weight loss alone proves sustained ketosis.
- The liked 80→59 cohort transition is retained. Missing measurement is explicitly distinguished from dietary non-adherence.
- The independent ITT page and terminology are removed from slides and spoken notes. Missing-outcome handling is described directly.
- No standalone improvements, generalisability section, name/UID placeholder, narration or embedded video.

## Pacing and script

### 01. Why this design deserves credit. — 00:00–00:25

Four features strengthen the comparison. Stratified randomisation helps balance sex and BMI. Concealed allocation reduces the opportunity to select participants knowing their next group. Shared workshops and coaching make unequal attention a less likely explanation. Calibrated weighing reduces reliance on participants’ own weight reports.

### 02. Advice did not guarantee equal intake. — 00:25–00:39

Both groups received energy-restriction advice; HKD additionally restricted carbohydrates. But actual calorie intake was not experimentally matched. At six months, reported calorie intake had fallen more in HKD.

### 03. Composition and calories both changed. — 00:39–00:53

So two things changed: diet composition and reported calorie intake. Greater weight loss could involve either, or both. Their separate contributions were not experimentally isolated.

### 04. A programme benefit is not a mechanism. — 00:53–01:05

If HKD helps people eat less, that is a programme benefit. But this study cannot show a ketosis-specific advantage independent of calories.

### 05. Was ketosis actually confirmed? — 01:05–01:17

HKD prescribed fifty grams or less of net carbohydrate daily. Food diaries describe reported intake, but they cannot establish who achieved sustained nutritional ketosis.

### 06. Weight loss does not prove ketosis. — 01:17–01:30

No objective ketone verification was reported. Weight loss cannot confirm ketosis either, because lower calorie intake can also cause it. These explanations could overlap.

### 07. Who stays in the picture? — 01:30–01:35

Now consider follow-up: eighty participants entered.

### 08. Who stays in the picture? — 01:35–01:45

Fifty-nine completed six-month measurements; twenty-one weights were unavailable. Missing measurement does not mean someone stopped following the diet.

### 09. The missing weights could matter. — 01:45–01:59

In HKD, twelve of forty-one participants lacked six-month measurements. They could have lost less, the same, or more weight than those measured. Their unknown outcomes might therefore change the group estimate.

### 10. Missing weights may bias the result. — 01:59–02:20

For example, if poorer HKD responders were missing, the measured group could look more successful. Other missingness patterns could shrink the advantage. This is potential attrition bias, not an established explanation of these departures. The report does not clearly explain how missing outcomes were handled.

### 11. Useful evidence, with clear limits. — 02:20–02:30

Strong features support the programme comparison; unverified mechanisms and missing outcomes limit how confidently we explain its advantage.

## Why the strengths matter

- Stratified randomisation: balancing sex and BMI reduces a known source of baseline differences. It does not guarantee identical groups on every characteristic.
- Concealed allocation: keeping the next assignment hidden reduces opportunities to influence which participants enter each arm. This is different from participant or assessor blinding, which is not claimed here.
- Shared support: both arms had workshops, app access and dietitian coaching, making an obvious difference in attention a less likely explanation. This does not prove contact was identical in every detail.
- Calibrated weighing: the primary outcome is less dependent on participants’ own reports of weight. This does not make the open-label trial fully blinded or remove every possible bias.

## Scientific interpretation

The energy figures are self-reported mean changes from baseline, not prescribed calories or absolute intake. The paper did not describe controlled feeding that ensured isocaloric exposure. Higher protein/fat energy percentages do not demonstrate that removed carbohydrate calories were fully replaced. A lower reported intake could mediate the programme effect; it does not automatically mean failed randomisation. Even an isocaloric comparison would not uniquely establish ketosis without verifying the metabolic state and accounting for other differences.

No objective ketone verification is reported. This is uncertainty about exposure and mechanism, not evidence that nobody achieved ketosis or that the entire programme failed. No appetite mechanism is presented as a demonstrated finding.

Missing six-month weight does not establish poor adherence. The 21 unobserved outcomes could belong to people following the diet, people not following it, or both. The bias example is conditional: if poorer HKD responders are disproportionately missing, the observed HKD result may look better. Other patterns could shrink the apparent advantage. The actual direction cannot be established from this report. The missing-data handling is not clearly described.
