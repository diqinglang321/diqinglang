### 01. A fairer comparison. · 00:00–00:11

Randomisation within sex and BMI strata helps balance those characteristics. Concealed allocation reduces the opportunity to select participants knowing their next group.

### 02. A clearer signal. · 00:11–00:22

Both groups received support, making unequal attention a less likely explanation. Calibrated weighing makes the primary outcome less dependent on self-report.

### 03. More weight loss. But why? · 00:22–00:26

First, the weight-loss mechanism was not isolated.

### 04. Two diets. Different intake reductions. · 00:26–00:36

Both groups received calorie-restriction advice. Yet reported intake fell by seven hundred and four calories in HKD, versus four hundred and twenty-three in ERD.

### 05. Assigned does not mean verified. · 00:36–00:46

Low-carbohydrate advice was intended to induce ketosis. But without objective ketone measurements, we cannot confirm who achieved or sustained that state.

### 06. Two possible routes. One result. · 00:46–00:58

Lower energy intake could also explain extra weight loss. These routes can overlap: ketosis, if present, might affect appetite. Their separate contributions were not established.

### 07. A programme effect. An unresolved mechanism. · 00:58–01:07

Eating less can be part of the programme’s benefit. The limitation is attributing that benefit specifically to ketosis, independently of calorie intake.

### 08. 80 people entered. · 01:07–01:11

Second, eighty people entered the trial.

### 09. 59 weights were available. · 01:11–01:18

At six months, fifty-nine had weight measurements. Twenty-one outcomes were missing, which does not establish dietary non-adherence.

### 10. Zoom in on HKD. · 01:18–01:26

In HKD, twelve of forty-one weights were missing. These participants might still have followed the diet.

### 11. Their weight loss is unknown. · 01:26–01:36

They could have lost less, the same, or more weight than those measured. Without their outcomes, their contribution to the group estimate remains unknown.

### 12. Who is missing changes what we see. · 01:36–01:48

If poorer HKD responders were disproportionately missing, the observed subset would look more successful. A comparison based on that subset could overstate the benefit: potential attrition bias.

### 13. The direction is unknown. · 01:48–01:56

Other missingness patterns could shrink the contrast. The report does not clearly explain how missing outcomes were handled.

### 14. One setting. A selected sample. · 01:56–02:06

Third, this was one hospital’s staff, about eighty-six percent women, with English and smartphone requirements. Random assignment does not make the sample representative.

### 15. The support was part of the setting. · 02:06–02:14

Participants also received seven workshops and app-based dietitian coaching. That support formed part of the conditions under which the result was obtained.

### 16. Same benefit elsewhere? · 02:14–02:22

Other adults, or those receiving less support, may respond differently. Similar benefit is untested, rather than proven absent.

### 17. Three limits. Three questions. · 02:22–02:30

So the design leaves three questions: why it works, how reliable the estimated benefit is, and for whom it applies.

## Evidence and interpretation

The four strengths evaluate specific threats: stratified randomisation helps balance sex and BMI; concealed allocation limits selection opportunities; support in both arms limits unequal attention as an explanation; calibrated clinic weighing reduces reliance on self-reported weight.

The calorie numbers are reported **changes from baseline** at six months, not prescribed or absolute energy intakes. Both arms received energy-restriction advice, but the report does not describe controlled feeding that held actual intake equal. Lower intake can mediate the programme effect, so the difference does not automatically imply failed randomisation or invalidate the total programme comparison. Ketosis was not objectively verified; that does not establish that nobody achieved it. Ketosis and intake pathways may overlap, and no appetite effect or causal mediation was demonstrated. Equal calories alone would not uniquely prove ketosis as the mechanism.

The pictograms are exact: 80 randomised; 59 six-month weights (HKD29/41, ERD30/39); 21 missing (HKD12, ERD9). A missing weight is not proof of diet non-adherence. The poor-responder scenario is explicitly hypothetical and concerns the risk in a comparison based on measured participants. Missing outcomes could distort the contrast in either direction; the paper does not clearly explain their handling. No individual weight values, imputed outcomes, bias magnitudes or statistical tests were invented. There is no independent ITT or improvements section.

Generalisability concerns healthcare staff at one Singapore hospital; 69/80 were women, rounded to 86%; English literacy and smartphone access were required, and some diseases were excluded. The programme included seven workshops and app-based dietitian support for six months. Effects in other people or less-supported conditions are not established. Limited applicability does not demonstrate lack of benefit elsewhere, nor does random assignment make recruitment representative.
